### Reading: does prioritized FEC earn its 17 percent?

The class A half earns it; the rest does not, at any headroom measured.  Across 8 scenarios spanning 0 to 70 % headroom and 0 to 10 % link loss, class A parity alone (10.1 pp of wire overhead) left fewer tiles concealed than no parity at all in 7 of them, averaging 13.9 tiles per frame saved and peaking at 31.8.  The class B row on top of it helped in 0 of 8: it cost tiles in every other scenario, up to 45.0 per frame, for a further 3.5 pp of overhead.  The full 30 / 10 / 0 policy beat no-parity in only 2 of 8.

The mechanism is the band deadline, not the coding.  Parity is extra bytes in the same band window as the data it protects, and the per-band burst is several times the average rate, so the parity for band b delays band b+1 into its own deadline.  Class A parity is cheap enough that the datagrams it recovers outnumber the ones it delays; the class B row is not.  The reference-age columns show the same edge from the other side: on the 300 Mbit/s link, parity off holds 33.2 % of tiles on N-1 and the full policy holds 1.1 %, so the parity bytes also cost nearly every tile a frame of reference recency, which PAPER 6.6 prices at a further 5 to 10 % of bits.  That second-order cost is not in the paper's budget at all.

Two false leads are worth recording, because both looked like results.  The first version of this sweep ran against the v1 deadline controller, whose climb rule has a dead zone: a band that is systematically a millisecond late is about 6 % of a frame, under the 10 % miss threshold, so the deadline never moved and those tiles stayed concealed forever.  That inflated the parity-off column on fast links to 153.7 tiles per frame and made FEC look like a large win there.  With decision D24 in place the same row is 38.4, and the apparent win disappears.  The second was a headroom-gated ladder built on that inflated control: it turned the class B row on above 50 % headroom and escalated parity on measured loss, which on a link whose loss is mostly congestion loss caused by its own parity is a positive feedback loop.  It spent 25.9 % of the wire and concealed more than either fixed setting.  Both are why the shipped default is the plain one: class A parity at the nominal ratio, no class B or C, no loss escalation.

Caveats that still stand.  A concealed tile is not a lost tile -- it is a warp of the previous frame, nearly free in the periphery and very visible in the fovea -- so a tile count understates class A parity and overstates class B, and the sweep should be repeated against VMAF or the foveated metric of PAPER 5.3 before the class B row is removed from the syntax rather than merely from the default.  And the intra column is the GRACE comparison in one number: it stays near zero in every row, with or without parity, because a concealed tile remains an exact reference (decisions D10 and D17), so a loss costs one frame of prediction rather than an undecodable frame.  That structural resilience is what GRACE trains a network to acquire and what NX Warp gets from the reference model for free -- and it, not the concealment counts, is the strongest argument here for spending less on FEC.

### Why the numbers differ

**Datagrams per frame.** v2 took the per-tile class and reference out of the
header (decision D19), so a run only breaks at a tile row, a layer or the
payload budget.  Runs went from 9.0 to 11.3 tiles and the rate from 291 to 245
datagrams per frame.  The paper's 150 is still out of reach and always was: it
assumes 20 average tiles in 1800 payload bytes, but 20 tiles of 90 bytes plus
their directory entries is 1880 bytes, which no 1400-byte MTU can hold.  The
arithmetic ceiling is 14 average tiles per run, and the heavy tail (fovea tiles
at roughly 3x the mean) pulls the achieved mean to 11.3.  Reaching the paper's
number needs a jumbo MTU, which is exactly what PAPER 4.1 offers on USB.

**Overhead.** The paper's 5.5 %% is `(24 + 20*4) / (104 + 1800)`.  It omits the
16-byte AEAD tag its own section 4.1 mandates, the 26-byte pose header its
section 6.7 replicates per band, and IPv4/UDP.  v2 brings the header and
directory share from 8.7 %% to 7.8 %%; adding the tag it is 9.6 %%, and 23.3 %%
with FEC and IPv4/UDP.  The irreducible part is the 4-byte directory entry,
which is 4.3 %% of a 90-byte tile on its own.

**FEC.** v1 measured 20.9 %% against the paper's 14.5 %% because a fixed 3 / 1 / 0
parity per group is 100 %% overhead on a group of three, and because parity
blocks are padded to the longest member.  v2 scales the parity with the realised
k (decision D23) and groups by descending length (D22), which brings it to
17.1 %%.  The remaining 2.6 points over the paper are structural: groups may not
cross a band, so every band ends with a short class A group that still pays its
one-block floor, and a run carrying a single fovea tile is protected as fovea.
Closing the gap would mean letting class A groups span the frame, which trades
the band deadline for parity, or dropping the floor and accepting unprotected
fovea runs.

**Band latency and reference distance.** The paper's 4.2 timeline gives the air
3 ms and no serialisation time at all: 208 KB at 300 Mbit/s takes 5.5 ms to
clock out, which is most of a 11.1 ms frame.  At 300 Mbit/s of usable air rate
the queue never empties inside a frame, the deadline controller saturates at its
4 ms limit, and feedback for frame N arrives too late for frame N+1, so 97.6 %%
of tiles reference N-2 rather than the bottom bands only.  Give the link 600
Mbit/s and band latency drops to 7.5 ms and 99.6 %% of tiles reference N-1.  The
paper's latency and reference-distance claims are claims about a link with about
3x headroom over the stream, and should say so.

**Deadline direction.** PAPER 4.3 says the deadline "moves 1 ms earlier ...
trading latency for fewer holes".  Moving it earlier gives less time for
datagrams and therefore more holes.  Decision D16 implements it as moving later,
which is what the stated trade means; with the paper's sign the simulator
conceals every tile of every frame on the 300 Mbit/s link.

**Late tiles.** PAPER 4.3 item 5 makes a late tile a valid reference.  Decision
D17 decodes it for display but does not acknowledge it, because otherwise the
client holds pixels the sender's shadow believes are concealed and prediction
diverges silently - exactly what 4.5 exists to prevent.  With D17 the shadow is
bit-exact in every scenario and every fuzz seed; without it the simulator shows
thousands of divergent tiles per run.

**Repaired bytes.** The parenthesised figure is the part of a repair whose
original never arrived; the difference is wasted GF(256) work.  v1 repaired a
group as soon as k blocks were in hand, which rebuilds any group whose members
were merely reordered.  How much that costs depends entirely on the link model:
with the earlier per-datagram jitter model it was 19.9 MB per ten seconds at
zero loss, and with the order-preserving A-MPDU model used here it is close to

The link model itself was corrected in the course of this revision: 802.11
delivers an A-MPDU in order, so jitter belongs to channel access once per
aggregate, not to each datagram independently.  The v1 figures in the table
above are re-measured under the corrected model, which is why they differ from
the ones in the first version of this file.

**Class A duplication.** The paper prices duplication at the class A share of
*codec* bits.  On the wire a duplicated datagram also duplicates its 24-byte
header, its 16-byte tag and its share of parity, so 32.6 %% of codec bits costs
41 %% of wire bytes.  The striper's headroom test (section 10) is written in
wire bytes for exactly this reason, so it does not over-commit the link; the
paper's budget line should be too.

**USB.** The 20 to 50 ms RNDIS/NCM stalls of PAPER 4.11 fill a 512 KB socket
buffer in about 21 ms at this rate, so a single-path USB user loses a burst to
tail drop rather than riding the stall out.  Multipath answers it: with WiFi as
the second path, late tiles fall from 5.6 to 3.8 per frame and band latency p50
is 4.9 ms.
